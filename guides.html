7 signs a backtest is too good to be true

Most bot sellers are not lying on purpose. Their backtest simply was not built to find problems. These are the signs we see most often.

  1. No fees, or tiny fees. A bot that trades often can lose its whole edge to fees. Ask what fee per trade was used; on Binance spot it is about 0.1% per side.
  2. A short, hand-picked period. Three months of a bull market makes almost any long-only bot look brilliant. Ask for results across a rise, a fall and a sideways market.
  3. A perfectly smooth equity curve. Real trading has bad weeks. A curve with no drawdowns usually means a mistake, or settings tuned to fit the past exactly.
  4. Very few trades. With 20 trades, luck and skill look the same. Under about 100 trades, treat any result as a hint.
  5. Win rate is the headline. A 90% win rate means little if the losing 10% wipes out the rest. Look at profit factor and maximum drawdown instead.
  6. No comparison with simply holding. If holding the same coins made more money, the bot added risk, not value.
  7. It only works with one exact setting. If changing one number a little breaks it, it was fitted to the past.

What is lookahead bias, and why it fools so many bots

Lookahead bias means the strategy uses information it could not have had at the moment it traded. The classic example: deciding to buy at the open of a candle using that candle's close. In a backtest the close is already in the data, so the decision looks brilliant. Live, the close is still in the future, and the edge disappears.

It hides in ordinary code: indicators calculated over the whole dataset at once, signals shifted the wrong way, or "repainting" indicators on TradingView that redraw their past values. The strategy's author usually has no idea.

The test is simple in principle: run the strategy again with the future cut off, candle by candle, and see whether its signals change. If they do, it is peeking. One of the free strategies in our showcase looked profitable over five years until this check caught it.

How to check a seller's track record before you pay

  1. Ask for the trade list, not screenshots. Every trade with its coin, side, entry and exit time and price. A seller with real results can export it from the exchange in a minute.
  2. Check that every price really traded. An entry far from any price the market reached that minute is a red flag (small differences between exchanges are normal). We check each price against real exchange data, down to the second when the times have seconds.
  3. Add the real costs. Fees, funding for futures, and some slippage. Claimed profits are almost always before costs.
  4. Look at the whole period. Ask for every trade since the bot started, not a selection.
  5. Prefer verifiable reports. A report anyone can check, like ours with its QR code, cannot be quietly edited.

Tools worth knowing

We are not the only way to check a bot. These are good tools for different jobs:

  • Myfxbook and FX Blue: follow a live Forex account and confirm its history matches the broker. Great for live results; they do not backtest.
  • Freqtrade lookahead analysis: if you write Freqtrade strategies yourself, run it on every strategy before trusting a backtest.
  • TradingView strategy tester: quick first look at a Pine strategy. Watch out for repainting and set realistic fees.
